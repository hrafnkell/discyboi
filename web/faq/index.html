Questions

The ones that keep coming up. Something missing? Email info@discyboi.com.

The device

How much does it weigh?

About 10 g with the case and the coin cell. That's a few percent of a disc, so it nudges the flight a little; mounting it on a disc a bit lighter than your usual one keeps the total close to what you're used to.

Why two motion sensors?

Mostly for spin. The gyro tops out at about 650 rpm, and a decent backhand spins at 800–1,400 rpm. Above that, the sensor 9 mm off the centre feels a pull towards the centre that grows with spin, so spin can be worked out from it instead.

On its own that sensor can't tell the pull apart from the throw's own acceleration or gravity. The centre sensor feels those too but almost no pull, so subtracting one from the other leaves a clean spin reading. The centre sensor also gives the disc's own motion for the release speed, and the pair shows how far off centre the board is mounted.

Does it work on very domey discs?

It should. The case has a flat base and sits on 0.6–1.1 mm VHB tape, which takes up the slight curve under the flight plate; on a typical disc that's under 0.1 mm across the case. On a strongly domed disc, use the thicker tape. The centring jig works from the injection mark, so the dome doesn't affect it.

Can I move it between discs?

Yes. Glue a printed base onto each disc you want to use, then undo the three lid screws and move the board and the lid across. A spare base is a few grams of plastic and a piece of tape. The board keeps its calibration, which is stored on the board itself.

Can I use it to find my disc?

No. It has no GPS or beeper, and Bluetooth only reaches a few tens of metres.

Using it

What do I use it for?

Mostly throwing into a net. Everything is measured in the pull and the release, so 1–2 m of flight is enough. The lab then flies the throw as a full simulated flight with the disc you pick, so you see roughly where it would have gone. It's made for working on form: every throw of a session, trends, and comparisons.

How fast does a throw show up?

Basically immediately. The board works out the numbers on the disc and sends them when the disc hits the net, and the simulated flight starts straight away.

Does it work on an iPhone or iPad?

Not in Safari. Apple doesn't support Web Bluetooth in any iOS browser, so the page can't reach the board. Apps like Bluefy or WebBLE add Web Bluetooth to iOS and might work, but I haven't tested them. Chrome or Edge on a laptop, a desktop or an Android phone all work; there's nothing to install.

How much does centring matter?

It matters, but less than you might think. The centre sensor should sit on the spin axis: off centre, the spin adds a pull that looks like the disc accelerating. The firmware accounts for small offsets and learns the sensors' own quirks over your first few throws. The printed jig centres the case on the disc's injection mark, which is close enough.

What does the lab record about my board?

To count how many boards exist, the lab sends the server an anonymous board id each time it connects and each time you throw. The id is a scrambled (hashed) form of the chip's factory serial number, sent with the firmware version. Nothing about you goes with it, and you don't need an account. Throws are only stored on the server if you log in and save them.

How do I update the firmware?

In the lab: connect, then choose Update firmware. It goes over Bluetooth, with no cables.

Accuracy and the simulator

How accurate is it?

Good enough to work on form, but not yet checked against a radar or a camera.

  • Spin is measured directly and is the most reliable number.
  • Speed comes from adding up the acceleration through the pull. Small errors build up over the 1–2 s that takes, so it's the number most likely to be off.
  • Hyzer and nose angle come from tracking the disc's orientation with the gyro from the moment it's still in your hand to release.
  • Distance is a simulation of those numbers with the disc you choose, so it's an estimate of where the throw would have gone, not a measurement.
Has it been calibrated against a known source?

Not yet. So far it has been checked by hundreds of real throws and by whether the numbers make sense for those throws. A comparison with a radar gun or a high-speed camera is the next step; if you have one and would like to help, get in touch.

Where do the simulator's lift, drag and moment curves come from?

From the standard published disc models (Hummel 2003; Potts and Crowther 2002). Lift, drag and pitching moment depend on the angle between the airflow and the disc, and the spinning disc is simulated in full 3D, so the pitching moment tips it sideways rather than nose up. That's what makes discs turn and fade, and why more spin makes them steadier.

A disc's flight numbers set the curves: speed lowers drag, glide adds lift, turn adds a nose-down moment at high speed, and fade a nose-up one as it slows. Flight numbers are opinions, not measurements, so the mapping is tuned to give typical distances for a given arm speed rather than measured in a wind tunnel.

Getting one and building one

Where do I get one?

Nothing is for sale yet. Sign up if you'd like an assembled board from the first small batch, or build one yourself: the files, parts list and cost are all there.

Can I use the website with a board I built myself?

Yes, the lab works with any discyboi board. To get firmware updates from the website, flash the released firmware (see first flash). Builds signed with your own key work too, but then the website's updates won't install on them.

Will it work with sim software like Link Disc Golf?

Not today: discyboi talks to its own lab. Its Bluetooth protocol is open and defined in the source, so a link to other software is possible.

Is this made with AI?

Yes, substantially. Much of the firmware, the website and the board design code were written with an AI coding assistant. I designed it, directed the work, and built and tested it on real boards with hundreds of throws.
