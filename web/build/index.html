Build one

Everything needed to make one is open source and in the repository. This page walks through it: order the board and parts, solder them, flash the firmware once with a cheap RP2040 board, print the case and stick it on a disc. After that first flash, firmware updates come over Bluetooth from the browser.

The board

A round 34 mm, 4-layer board with a Fanstel BC15M Bluetooth module (nRF54L15) and two ST LSM6DSV320X motion sensors, one at the centre and one 9 mm out, so spin can be measured beyond the gyro's limit. It runs from a CR2032 in a clip on the back.

Passives are generic: any brand that meets the spec will do; the part numbers are just ones that fit. Two catches: use a 6.3 V or higher cap for the 100 µF (a 4 V one keeps far less of its capacitance at 3 V), and a yellow-green (AlInGaP) LED, not a "true green" one, which barely lights from 3 V through 1 kΩ. The module, the sensors, the regulator and the clip must be the exact parts.

Leave empty: U4, R7, R8, R9, D2, D3 and C13 are a LiPo charger, only for a rechargeable build. Never fit them with a CR2032 in the clip. The Bluetooth module, the motion sensors and the 10 MΩ resistors have no LCSC code: for JLCPCB assembly, get them through JLCPCB's Global Sourcing or send them in as consigned parts.

Also needed: a CR2032 cell; for the case, 3 × M2 × 6 mm countersunk screws and 3M VHB tape (0.6–1.1 mm); for the first flash, an RP2040-Zero and four thin wires (see below). J2 and the four square pads are bare copper, nothing to buy.

Order and assemble

  1. Order the boards. Upload the gerbers to JLCPCB: 4 layers, 1.0 mm thick (the case assumes it), lead-free HASL or ENIG, and a top-side stencil, 0.10 mm. The minimum order is 5.
  2. Paste and place. Stencil paste onto the top side and place the parts. The placement map shows where each one goes and which way round, with tick-boxes as you go. The two motion sensors are 0.5 mm pitch: press each one down level after placing it.
  3. Reflow on a hot plate (the profile is in the placement map), then check the sensors and the module for bridges. The BC15M is an LGA: it needs paste and reflow, an iron alone won't do.
  4. Battery clip. Solder its two tabs on the bottom with an iron. Keep the round negative pad flat: leave it as delivered on HASL or ENIG.

First flash

A new board is flashed once over SWD. A Waveshare RP2040-Zero (a few dollars) running Raspberry Pi's debugprobe firmware does the job; a Raspberry Pi Pico works the same way. After this, updates come over Bluetooth: connect the disc in the lab and it offers new firmware when there is some.

You need

  • RP2040-Zero (or a Pico) and a USB cable. Waveshare wiki
  • debugprobe firmware: debugprobe_on_pico.uf2 from raspberrypi/debugprobe releases. It turns the RP2040 into a CMSIS-DAP probe.
  • probe-rs, 0.32 or newer (it knows the nRF54L15). Install probe-rs
  • The firmware: discyboi-full.hex, the bootloader and the app in one file, from the latest firmware release.
  • Four thin wires (30 AWG) and a fine soldering tip, or a Tag-Connect TC2030-CTX-NL cable.

Wiring

J2 is the six-pad SWD footprint at the top left of the top side. Seen from the top, flat edge up:

Power the board from the RP2040 or from a coin cell, never both: the 3.3 V would feed back into the CR2032. NRESET isn't needed. The GP numbers are the ones printed on the RP2040-Zero, the same pins a Pico uses (Pico pins 4 and 5).

Flash it

  1. Hold the RP2040-Zero's BOOT button while plugging it in. It shows up as a drive: copy debugprobe_on_pico.uf2 onto it. It restarts as a debug probe.
  2. Wire it to J2 as above, with no cell in the clip.
  3. Run, in the folder with discyboi-full.hex: 
    probe-rs erase --chip nRF54L15 --allow-erase-all      # once: a new nRF54L15 is locked
probe-rs download --chip nRF54L15 --binary-format hex discyboi-full.hex
probe-rs reset --chip nRF54L15
  4. The green LED blinks 3 times: both motion sensors answered. Ten fast blinks means one didn't: check its solder joints.
  5. Unplug the probe, remove the wires, put a CR2032 in and open the lab in Chrome or Edge. Connect disc finds it as discyboi.

From here on, updates are over Bluetooth. When there's newer firmware, the lab shows Update firmware next to the Connect button. The image is checked and signed, and if anything goes wrong the disc keeps the firmware it has. (If you build the firmware yourself with firmware/build.sh, flash your own merged.hex instead: your board will then only take updates signed with your key, so the lab's updates won't apply to it.)

Case and mounting

  • Print the base and lid in PETG or TPU (not PLA, it cracks in the cold): 0.2 mm layers, 4 perimeters, 100 % infill, no supports. A translucent base lets the LED show through.
  • Screws: 3 × M2 × 6 mm countersunk. They self-tap into the base; drive them gently.
  • Board: components face the disc, battery faces the ground. In the lab, set the board mount to Face up.
  • Mount: clean the flight plate with isopropyl alcohol and stick the base on with 3M VHB tape (0.6–1.1 mm). Centre it with the jig: the sight finds the injection mark in the middle of the disc, so it works on any disc.

Roughly US$30–35 in parts per tracker. The two motion sensors and the Bluetooth module are most of it.

Rough single-unit prices from Mouser and JLCPCB, not quotes. If you're just building one, you need a minimum order of 5 PCBs, add shipping and realistically the cost would be around $90 USD. You need to be comfortable reflow-soldering very tiny 0.5 mm-pitch parts.
